\begin{abstract}
We outline a practical baseline for turning \texttt{GRTeclyn} into a closed-loop search engine for exotic spacetime metrics on a GPU cluster. Building on prior 3D numerical-relativity evolutions of the Ellis--Bronnikov wormhole, we present an automated framework that goes beyond static metric inspection. A Python scheduler proposes parameter vectors representing spatial and extrinsic curvature profiles. We outline two primary pathways to handle the matter sector: directly reconstructing the required matter distribution from the Einstein constraint equations at $t=0$, or employing an elliptic solver to force the starting geometry to be mathematically consistent with standard, positive-energy matter. The system is designed to run in batches on a GPU cluster, using early-stopping filters to optimize node hours. Promoted candidates are subjected to operational faster-than-light (FTL) tests, a resolution convergence ladder, and physical stability checks to distinguish physical FTL dynamics from coordinate artifacts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Most proposed interstellar metrics are designed by hand. One writes a static, highly symmetric metric ansatz, calculates the resulting stress-energy tensor using the Einstein field equations, and inspects whether it possesses FTL or shortcut properties. This traditional method is biased toward highly simplified, static geometries that humans know how to write down in closed form.

By treating metric discovery as a closed-loop optimization problem, we can search a much wider, non-spherical, and dynamical space of initial data. The previous \texttt{GRTeclyn} wormhole study demonstrated that 3D numerical relativity is highly suited for tracking the nonlinear evolution, instability, and collapse of exotic geometries. 

Rather than inspecting static equations, the proposed framework couples a Python-based optimization engine with the high-performance C++ GPU-accelerated AMR engine of \texttt{GRTeclyn}. This document outlines the physical, mathematical, and computational framework required to run this search engine across a GPU cluster, with a strict emphasis on deriving the matter sector and avoiding coordinate artifacts.

Throughout this draft, ``FTL'' means effective faster-than-light propagation through a curved spacetime geometry compared with flat-spacetime light travel. It does not imply local superluminal motion relative to the local light cone.

\section{Goal}

The objective is to construct an automated numerical-relativity laboratory for exotic spacetime metrics. The pipeline searches for geometries that display four classes of physical behavior:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{FTL transport:} a compact passenger region moves effectively faster than a light signal in flat spacetime, while maintaining bounded, survivable tidal forces.
    \item \textbf{FTL communication:} a physical signal (null ray or scalar pulse) arrives at a detector earlier than it would in a flat-spacetime control run.
    \item \textbf{Portals:} two distinct mouth-like structures create a stable shortcut.
    \item \textbf{Wormholes:} a throat-like spatial bridge that survives for multiple light-crossing times without immediately collapsing into a horizon.
\end{enumerate}

To ensure physical viability, the ultimate success criterion is to find stable, non-collapsing FTL geometries that minimize—or entirely eliminate—violations of the standard energy conditions.

\subsection{Optimization as the Engine of Discovery}

A foundational question is why a project aimed at the \emph{discovery} of novel, unclassified spacetimes relies so heavily on numerical \emph{optimization}. The answer lies in the dimensionality and physical constraints of the search space.

The configuration space of all possible 3D initial geometries—defined by the spatial metric $\gamma_{ij}$ and the extrinsic curvature $K_{ij}$—is infinitely dimensional. If one conducts a blind, unguided search (i.e., random sampling), the probability of finding a viable candidate is vanishingly small. Virtually all randomly generated initial metrics fail immediately: they either violate the Einstein constraints, collapse into immediate singularity horizons, or disperse into trivial flat space. 

Optimization provides the steering mechanism necessary to navigate this infinite space. By defining a strict objective scoring function—such as maximizing FTL signal arrival time while minimizing tidal forces and penalizing negative energy density—we establish a physics-guided fitness landscape. 

The optimizer does not merely "tweak" a pre-existing geometry; instead, it acts as an evolutionary sculptor. Using a flexible basis of radial and angular functions (e.g., spherical harmonics) as its raw material, the optimizer dynamically combines these modes. Through this process, the system can discover highly non-trivial, asymmetric, or rotating geometries—such as multi-mouth portals or stabilized warp-like corridors—that have never been written down in closed-form by hand. In this framework, optimization is not merely parameter tuning; it is the mathematical mechanism of topological and geometric discovery.

\section{Initial Data and Matter Derivation}

In general relativity, we cannot prescribe an arbitrary initial geometry without satisfying the Einstein constraint equations at $t=0$. The initial spatial metric $\gamma_{ij}$ and the extrinsic curvature $K_{ij}$ are mathematically coupled to the matter's local energy density $\rho$ and momentum density $j_i$.

We propose two distinct pathways to handle the matter sector within the automated search loop:

\subsection{Path A: Algebraic Matter Reconstruction}

In this pathway, the optimizer is permitted to generate any arbitrary spatial geometry. We do not assume a specific matter model. Instead, we use the Einstein equations to algebraically derive the physical "cost" of the proposed geometry. 

Before the time evolution starts, \texttt{GRTeclyn} evaluates the Hamiltonian and Momentum constraints at every point on the 3D grid. We algebraically reconstruct the required energy density $\rho$ and momentum density $j_i$ at $t=0$:

\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{1}{16\pi} \left( R + K^2 - K_{ij}K^{ij} \right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    j_i = \frac{1}{8\pi} D_j\left(K^j_i - \delta^j_i K\right).
\end{equation}

Here, $R$ is the 3D Ricci scalar and $D_j$ is the covariant derivative. The Python script reads this derived $\rho$ grid from the diagnostic files. If $\rho < 0$ in any region, the script flags that this geometry requires exotic matter and calculates the integrated volume of negative energy.

\subsection{Path B: Elliptic Constraint Solving}

If we wish to strictly forbid exotic matter from the start, we cannot simply guess a geometry and reconstruct $\rho$. Instead, we fix the matter sector to a standard, positive-energy model (such as a standard scalar field with $\rho \ge 0$). 

We then employ an elliptic initial-data constraint solver (such as \texttt{GRTresna}, designed to work with the AMReX grid). The optimizer proposes a "target" geometry, and the elliptic solver slightly deforms this target until it mathematically satisfies the constraints while coupled only to our positive-energy matter field. The resulting constraint-satisfying initial data is then passed to \texttt{GRTeclyn} for evolution.

\section{Candidate Parameterization}

To avoid setting up million-point grids directly from the optimizer, the Python script generates a compact parameter vector $\theta$. A decoder turns $\theta$ into smooth initial fields on the AMReX grid.

\subsection{Level 1: Radial Recipes}

We define the initial fields at $t=0$ using spherically symmetric radial profiles:
\begin{equation}
    q(r) = q_\infty + \sum_n a_n B_n(r),
\end{equation}
where $q$ represents the CCZ4 fields $\chi$, $\alpha$, $\beta^i$, or $K$. The coefficients $a_n$ are the parameters sampled by the optimizer, and $B_n(r)$ are smooth radial basis functions (such as Gaussians).

\subsection{Level 2: Angular Recipes}

To allow for non-spherical, asymmetric, or rotating geometries, we expand the parameterization using spherical harmonics:
\begin{equation}
    q(r,\theta,\varphi) = q_\infty + \sum_{n\ell m} a_{n\ell m}B_n(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi).
\end{equation}
This enables the search engine to discover rotating warp fields, quadrupolar configurations, and asymmetric wormhole mouths.

\subsection{Level 3: Neural Field Decoder}

In the advanced phase, we can parameterize the initial fields using an implicit coordinate-based neural network (such as a SIREN network):
\begin{equation}
    (x,y,z) \xrightarrow{\text{NN}(\theta)} \{\chi,\alpha,\beta^i,K,\tilde A_{ij},\phi,\Pi\}.
\end{equation}
This is the most flexible option but requires a stable training loop and will be implemented only after the Level 1 and Level 2 pipelines are thoroughly validated.

\subsection{Level 4: Hybrid Meta-Optimization via LLM Agents}

While Level 1 and Level 2 parameter searches are managed by derivative-free numerical optimizers (such as CMA-ES), we introduce a high-level LLM-based research agent to manage the meta-optimization loop. The LLM agent does not propose raw floating-point coefficients. Instead, it operates on a feedback loop utilizing the diagnostic output of completed batches:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Automated Diagnostics and Debugging:} If a cluster of candidates terminates early with solver failures or constraint explosions, the LLM agent parses the raw compilation and execution logs. It diagnoses whether the failure is physical (e.g., a physical singularity) or numerical (e.g., coordinate shear, boundary reflection, or grid resolution exhaustion) and modifies the CCZ4 gauge parameters or grid settings accordingly.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Hypothesis and Basis Selection:} If the numerical optimizer plateaus in a local minimum, the LLM agent generates new physical hypotheses. It instructs the Python scheduler to modify the parameterization, such as enabling specific angular modes, changing the scalar-field potential $V(\phi)$, or altering the initialization of the shift vector $\beta^i$.
    \item \textbf{Automated Code Adaptation:} For complex physical setups, the agent writes example-local C++ headers (e.g., custom potentials or initial-data functors), runs pre-flight compilation checks, and resolves compiler warnings before launching new search episodes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Execution Boundary}

The division of labor is designed to eliminate compile-time overhead:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Python Controller (CPU/Head Node):} Runs the optimization algorithm, prepares a batch of parameter vectors $\theta$, writes them to individual text-based \texttt{params.txt} inputs, and dispatches them as parallel tasks to the cluster's Slurm scheduler.
    \item \textbf{C++ Initial Data (GPU/Compute Nodes):} At $t=0$, the compiled \texttt{GRTeclyn} binary starts up on the assigned GPU, reads the coefficients from \texttt{params.txt}, and executes a custom initial-data GPU functor to populate the 3D AMReX grid with smooth initial fields.
    \item \textbf{C++ RHS Evolution (GPU/Compute Nodes):} At $t > 0$, the GPU runs the pre-compiled, highly optimized CCZ4 and matter RHS evolution routines (e.g. \texttt{CCZ4RHSWithMatter}). Because the general relativity and matter equations are pre-compiled, the search loop runs with zero compilation overhead.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tiered GPU Execution}

To prevent a massive search from exhausting cluster node hours, we implement a tiered system:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1 (Broad Exploration):} Each candidate is allocated to a single GPU and run at a low resolution (e.g. $N_{\text{full}} = 64$ with 2 levels of AMR) for a short duration. Early-stopping criteria instantly terminate a run if constraints explode, the lapse collapses everywhere, or the grid becomes unresolved.
    \item \textbf{Tier 2 (Deep Validation):} High-scoring candidates that survive Tier 1 are promoted to run on multi-GPU, multi-node configurations at high resolution ($N_{\text{full}} = 128 \to 256$, with up to 5 levels of AMR) to verify stability and convergence.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Closed-Loop: How Simulation Results Drive the Search}
\label{sec:closed_loop}

A common question is: where exactly does the feedback happen?  The answer is that every candidate geometry completes a full cycle through three stages before the optimizer sees its score.  This section traces one iteration of the loop end-to-end.

\subsection{One iteration, step by step}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Propose.}  The optimizer (CMA-ES or random sampler) emits a parameter vector $\theta = (c_0,c_1,\ldots,c_{N-1},w)$, where $c_n$ are Gaussian basis coefficients for $\chi(r)$ and $w$ is the shared basis width.

    \item \textbf{Constrain.}  The Python wrapper derives $\phi(r)$ from $\chi(r)$ via the Hamiltonian constraint (Sec.~\ref{sec:constrained_recipe}), locks $K=\mathrm{const}$ and $\Pi=0$, and writes the complete \texttt{params.txt}.

    \item \textbf{Pre-flight filter.}  A coarse 1D check evaluates $\|H\|_{L^2}$ and $\|M_i\|_{L^2}$. If the residuals exceed thresholds, the candidate is rejected \emph{without} launching a GPU job, saving node hours.

    \item \textbf{Simulate.}  The C++ binary \texttt{main3d.gnu.CUDA.ex} loads \texttt{params.txt}, populates the 3D AMReX grid, and evolves the CCZ4+matter system on the GPU for a fixed wall time or simulation time $t_{\max}$.

    \item \textbf{Diagnose.}  During evolution, \texttt{RadialRecipeLevel} writes two time-series files every $\Delta t_{\rm diag}$ steps:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \texttt{constraint\_norms.dat}: $\|H\|_{L^2}$, $\|M_i\|_{L^2}$, $\min/\max(\rho_{\rm req})$, integrated negative-$\rho$ volume;
        \item \texttt{collapse\_diagnostics.dat}: $\alpha_{\min}$, horizon-radius proxy, $|K|_{\max}$, scalar $\phi/\Pi$ ranges.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Score.}  The Python scorer parses both files and computes a scalar reward $S(\theta)$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:scoring}).  This single number summarizes: did the geometry survive? were constraints controlled? is the lapse healthy? does it need exotic matter? is the geometry non-trivial?

    \item \textbf{Update.}  The optimizer receives $S(\theta)$ (or $-S$ because CMA-ES minimizes).  After one full generation of $\lambda$ candidates, CMA-ES updates its internal mean and covariance matrix, shifting the search distribution toward higher-scoring regions.  The next generation's proposals $\theta'$ are drawn from this updated distribution.
\end{enumerate}

The loop repeats for $G$ generations (typically 50).  There is no gradient computation: the optimizer treats the entire simulate$\to$diagnose$\to$score chain as a black-box function $f(\theta) = -S(\theta)$.

\subsection{What the optimizer ``sees''}

From the optimizer's perspective, the physics engine is a noisy black box.  It sends in a vector of 5--20 real numbers and receives back one scalar.  It never sees the 3D grid, the constraint equations, or the matter fields directly.  All physical information is compressed into the score $S(\theta)$, which is why the scoring function design is critical (Sec.~\ref{sec:scoring}).

\section{Scoring: Overcoming the Flat-Space Attractor}
\label{sec:scoring}

The objective function must reward desirable physical outcomes and heavily penalize non-physical behavior or numerical errors.  However, a naive objective function leads to a degenerate global attractor at flat Minkowski space: flat space has zero constraint violation and perfect Eulerian stability ($s_{\rm stability}=1.0$), so any non-trivial warp or bend initially \emph{decreases} the total score, trapping the optimizer in a local minimum.

Two implementation biases cause this cold-start problem.  First, the static stability metric (Eq.~\ref{eq:delta_geom}) penalizes any change in grid variables over time from an Eulerian perspective, treating the smooth coordinate motion of a propagating warp bubble as an ``instability.''  Second, the raw FTL metrics ($F_{\rm null}$, $F_{\rm portal}$, $F_{\rm throat}$) are nearly binary: they reward the optimizer only after it achieves superluminal null-ray speeds or strong throat pinching, providing no gradient for weak, subluminal warp precursors.

To guide the search out of the flat-space valley, we upgrade the scoring architecture with three continuous geometric scaffolding terms: Nat\'{a}rio--Alcubierre expansion asymmetry ($F_{\rm asymmetry}$), logarithmic FTL amplification ($F_{\rm log}$), and co-moving stability ($s_{\rm comoving}$).

\subsection{Conceptual form}

At the highest level, the total score $S(\theta)$ for a candidate parameter set is:
\begin{equation}
    S(\theta) = w_1 F_{\text{FTL}} - w_2 \mathcal{H}_{\text{violation}} - w_3 E_{\text{exotic}} - w_4 T_{\text{tidal}},
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $F_{\text{FTL}}$ is the measured FTL shortcut benefit from $t=0$ profile integrals (Sec.~\ref{sec:ftl_scoring}); dynamic operational probes remain a Tier-2 validation step.
    \item $\mathcal{H}_{\text{violation}}$ is the integrated volume norm of the Hamiltonian constraint violation;
    \item $E_{\text{exotic}}$ is the integrated volume of negative energy density ($T_{00} < 0$);
    \item $T_{\text{tidal}}$ is the maximum tidal force felt by an observer in the passenger region (future).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implemented score components}

In the current codebase (\texttt{score.py}), every component $s_k$ is mapped through a bounded reward function:
\begin{equation}
    r(v;\,\sigma) = \frac{1}{1 + v/\sigma},
    \label{eq:bounded_reward}
\end{equation}
where $v\ge 0$ is the measured violation magnitude and $\sigma$ is a scale that controls how sharply the reward decays.  The value $r=1$ means perfect, $r=0.5$ means $v=\sigma$, and $r\to 0$ for $v\gg\sigma$.

The total score is a weighted sum over the active components:
\begin{equation}
    S(\theta) = \sum_{k} w_k\,s_k(\theta).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Continuous FTL scaffolding metrics}
\label{sec:ftl_scaffolding}

In addition to absolute null-ray transit times (Sec.~\ref{sec:ftl_scoring}), we implement two continuous proxies that reward the physical \emph{precursors} of FTL metrics before superluminal propagation is achieved.

\subsubsection{Nat\'{a}rio--Alcubierre expansion asymmetry}

As demonstrated in numerical warp studies, a moving warp bubble is characterized by contraction of space in front of the passenger region and expansion behind it.  On the $x$-axis we use a 1D expansion-scalar proxy $\theta(x)$ from shift divergence and conformal stretch, and define:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\rm asymmetry} = \max\!\left(0,\, \frac{\int_{x > 0} -\theta(\mathbf{x})\,d^3x + \int_{x < 0} \theta(\mathbf{x})\,d^3x}{\int |\theta(\mathbf{x})|\,d^3x + \epsilon_{\theta}} \right).
\end{equation}
In the implemented 1D profile evaluator, the volume integrals reduce to trapezoidal sums along the travel axis.  This term provides a smooth evolutionary ramp: the optimizer is rewarded for generating even weak, subluminal contraction/expansion pairs, guiding the search toward viable warp structures before $F_{\rm null}>0$.

\subsubsection{Logarithmic FTL amplification}

The linear coordinate travel-time metric $F_{\rm null}$ is very small for subluminal velocities (e.g.\ $F_{\rm null}\approx 0.063$ for $v_s=0.3c$; Table~\ref{tab:ftl_validation}).  To ensure weak shortcuts compete with flat-space stability rewards, we log-amplify the combined raw FTL score:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\rm log} = \frac{\ln(1 + \eta F_{\rm FTL})}{\ln(1 + \eta)},
    \label{eq:f_log}
\end{equation}
where $F_{\rm FTL}$ is the raw combined shortcut from Sec.~\ref{sec:ftl_scoring} and $\eta \approx 100$.  A $3\%$ shortcut maps to $F_{\rm log}\approx 0.46$, making it highly visible to the CMA-ES covariance matrix.

\subsection{Co-moving stability scoring}
\label{sec:comoving_scoring}

The static stability metric (Eq.~\ref{eq:delta_geom}) suffers from Eulerian bias: it penalizes any change in grid variables over time, but a physically viable propagating warp bubble \emph{must} alter local grid values as it moves.  To prevent the optimizer from selecting only stationary geometries, we evaluate stability in the co-moving frame of the bubble.

We estimate the average shift-vector velocity along the axis of travel from the $t=0$ recipe profile:
\begin{equation}
    \langle \beta^x \rangle = \frac{1}{V_{\rm bubble}} \int_{\rm bubble} \beta^x(\mathbf{x})\,d^3x.
\end{equation}
Using the shell-resolved $\chi(t)$ time series from \texttt{shell\_profiles.dat} (or \texttt{chi\_min}$(t)$ as fallback), we form a co-moving violation budget:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\rm comoving} = \frac{\max_t \left| \partial \chi / \partial t \right|}{\max(|\langle \beta^x \rangle|,\,10^{-3})},
    \qquad
    s_{\rm comoving} = \frac{1}{1 + \Delta_{\rm comoving}}.
    \label{eq:s_comoving}
\end{equation}
If a warp bubble propagates smoothly without internal shear, $s_{\rm comoving}\to 1$ even when Eulerian grid values change rapidly.  For \emph{stationary} throats and portals with $|\langle \beta^x \rangle|<\epsilon$ ($\epsilon=0.05$), the co-moving frame is undefined; \texttt{score.py} substitutes the Eulerian stability reward $s_{\rm stability}$ for $s_{\rm comoving}$, so static wormholes are not penalized for lacking a shift vector.  The legacy Eulerian term is retained at reduced weight ($w=0.5$) as an additional signal.

\subsection{Why these weights matter}

The weights $w_k$ define which physics the optimizer cares about.  For example:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $F_{\rm log}$ at weight $5.0$ amplifies even subluminal shortcuts so they compete with flat-space stability; without it, $F_{\rm null}=0.063$ contributes only $+0.32$ to $S$.
    \item $F_{\rm asymmetry}$ at weight $2.0$ rewards warp \emph{precursors} before superluminal transit is achieved, closing the cold-start gap.
    \item $s_{\rm comoving}$ at weight $2.5$ prevents propagating warp bubbles from being rejected by Eulerian stability alone (Sec.~\ref{sec:comoving_scoring}).
    \item Weights are user-configurable via \texttt{--score-weight} and \texttt{score.py}.
\end{itemize}

All weights are \emph{hyperparameters}, not learned parameters.  They are fixed before a search campaign begins and are not updated during optimization.

\subsection{FTL shortcut metrics (implemented)}
\label{sec:ftl_scoring}

Without an active FTL term, CMA-ES can maximize score via flat, stable spacetimes.  \texttt{ftl\_metrics.py} reconstructs $t=0$ profiles from recipe coefficients and evaluates three complementary shortcut indicators on the $x$-axis (integration limits $\pm L$).

\subsubsection{Warp null-ray shortcut}

For conformally flat spatial slices ($\tilde\gamma_{xx}=\chi^{-1}$), a null ray along $x$ has coordinate velocity
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dx}{dt} = \alpha(x)\sqrt{\chi(x)} - \beta^x(x).
\end{equation}
The coordinate travel time is
\begin{equation}
    T_{\rm curved} = \int_{-L}^{L}\frac{dx}{\alpha\sqrt{\chi}-\beta^x}, \qquad
    T_{\rm flat} = 2L,
\end{equation}
and the warp component is
\begin{equation}
    F_{\rm null} = \max\!\left(0,\,\frac{T_{\rm flat}-T_{\rm curved}}{T_{\rm flat}}\right).
\end{equation}
Paths with $\min(\alpha\sqrt{\chi}-\beta^x)$ below a floor are rejected (anti-fake-FTL guard).

\subsubsection{Portal proper-distance shortcut}

The proper distance along the axis is
\begin{equation}
    D_{\rm proper} = \int_{-L}^{L}\chi^{-1/2}(r)\,dr, \qquad
    F_{\rm portal} = \max\!\left(0,\,\frac{2L-D_{\rm proper}}{2L}\right),
\end{equation}
targeting compression bubbles with $\chi>1$ in the interior.

\subsubsection{Wormhole throat pinch}

Using $R_{\rm areal}=r/\sqrt{\chi}$ on the radial half-line, detect an interior local minimum $R_{\rm min}$ and compare to the asymptotic radius $R_{\rm asymp}$:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\rm throat} = \max\!\left(0,\,1-\frac{R_{\rm min}}{R_{\rm asymp}}\right).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Anti-flatness and combined objective}

Flat Minkowski must not win the search.  Define
\begin{equation}
    s_{\rm nonflat} = \min\!\left(1,\,\frac{\max|\chi-1|+\max|\beta^x|}{\tau}\right),
\end{equation}
with scale $\tau\sim 0.08$.  The optimizer-facing FTL reward is
\begin{equation}
    F_{\rm FTL} = \max(F_{\rm null},F_{\rm portal},F_{\rm throat})\cdot s_{\rm nonflat}.
\end{equation}
All $F$ components are rewards in $[0,1]$; \textbf{higher is better}.  The optimizer-facing FTL term is $F_{\rm log}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:f_log}), not the raw $F_{\rm FTL}$.  CMA-ES adds $5.0\,F_{\rm log}+2.0\,F_{\rm asymmetry}+1.0\,s_{\rm nonflat}$ to the total score.  The C++ initial-data module loads $\beta^x(r)$ via \texttt{recipe\_beta\_coeff\_*} (Gaussian basis), and the default search space includes $\alpha$ and $\beta$ coefficients alongside $\chi$.

\subsubsection{Why FTL scoring resolves the flat-space attractor}
\label{sec:ftl_attractor}

Without an active FTL objective, the CMA-ES fitness landscape contains a degenerate global attractor at flat Minkowski space.  This section explains the mechanism, its severity, and why the three-component $F_{\rm FTL}$ score eliminates it.

\paragraph{The problem: trivial geometry dominates.}
The non-FTL scoring components---survival, constraint health, lapse health, stability---are all maximized simultaneously by the Minkowski metric ($\alpha=1$, $\chi=1$, $\beta^x=0$, $K=0$): it survives to any $t_{\rm target}$, has zero constraint violation, maintains perfect lapse, and has $s_{\rm stability}=1$.  Its total non-FTL score is
\begin{equation}
    S_{\rm flat}^{(\text{no-FTL})} = 2.0 + 2.0 + 1.0 + 0 + 0 + 1.5 + 1.0 + 2.0 = 9.5.
\end{equation}
Any deformation away from flatness introduces constraint violation ($-2.0\,w_{\rm constr}$) and possible instability ($-2.0\,w_{\rm stab}$), yielding $S<9.5$.  Thus, without FTL pressure the optimizer converges to the trivial fixed point.

\paragraph{The solution: gradient toward structure.}
The optimizer now faces a \emph{positive gradient} pointing away from flatness toward FTL structure.  GPU smoke tests with the upgraded scorer (Table~\ref{tab:gpu_ftl_upgraded}) confirm that the flat-space attractor is broken: Alcubierre $v=0.5$ achieves $S=10.08$, exceeding flat Minkowski ($S=10.00$), because $F_{\rm log}=0.508$ and $s_{\rm comoving}=0.998$ compensate for low Eulerian stability ($s_{\rm stability}=0.02$).  Under the previous linear FTL term and Eulerian-only stability, the same run scored only $S=7.13$.

\paragraph{Three complementary channels prevent local minima.}
Each metric component rewards a geometrically distinct mechanism:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{$F_{\rm null}$ (shift-driven warp):} Sensitive to $\beta^x$-shaped bubbles that accelerate null rays.  Activated by increasing $|\texttt{recipe\_beta\_coeff}|$.
    \item \textbf{$F_{\rm portal}$ (spatial compression):} Sensitive to $\chi>1$ compression that shortens proper distance.  Activated by negative $\texttt{recipe\_chi\_coeff}$ (reducing conformal factor).
    \item \textbf{$F_{\rm throat}$ (topological pinch):} Sensitive to local minima in $R_{\rm areal}=r/\sqrt{\chi}$.  Activated by sharp $\chi$ peaks.
\end{enumerate}
Because these channels are independent (warp uses $\beta^x$; portal uses $\chi$ compression; throat uses $\chi$ peaks), the CMA-ES covariance matrix learns to explore them in parallel.  If one channel stalls due to stability penalties, the optimizer can shift probability mass to another.

\paragraph{The $s_{\rm nonflat}$ gate.}
The multiplicative anti-flatness factor $s_{\rm nonflat}\in[0,1]$ closes a secondary exploit.  Without it, a near-flat metric with tiny numerical rounding could produce $F_{\rm null}>0$ from discretization noise.  Because $s_{\rm nonflat}=0$ whenever $\max|\chi-1|+\max|\beta^x|<\tau=0.08$, a near-flat candidate is silenced regardless of its raw shortcut value.

\paragraph{Search-space coupling.}
The expanded CMA-ES search vector now contains $\alpha$ and $\beta^x$ coefficients (Table~\ref{tab:search_space}).  The optimizer can simultaneously shape the conformal factor for stability/constraints \emph{and} the shift for warp drive acceleration:
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \bigl(\underbrace{c_0^\chi, c_1^\chi, c_2^\chi, c_3^\chi}_{\text{geometry/throat}},\;\underbrace{w}_{\text{basis width}},\;\underbrace{c_0^\alpha, c_1^\alpha}_{\text{lapse shaping}},\;\underbrace{c_0^\beta, c_1^\beta}_{\text{warp drive}}\bigr).
\end{equation}
This nine-dimensional search space is the minimal parameterization that simultaneously spans warp, portal, and throat geometries while remaining tractable for CMA-ES population sizes $\lambda\sim20$.

\subsection{Dynamical stability scoring (implemented)}
\label{sec:stability_scoring}

The first AMR CMA-ES campaign exposed a \emph{dynamical stability gap}: candidates can complete a short $t=2$ run with high survival and constraint rewards while still collapsing or focusing in late-time $K$ slices.  Survival alone therefore cannot distinguish a true equilibrium from a geometry that collapses slowly enough to reach the stop time.  This is penalized explicitly in \texttt{metrics.py} and \texttt{score.py}.

\subsubsection{Data sources}

At each diagnostic time step the C++ evolution writes \texttt{collapse\_diagnostics.dat}, whose columns include the minimum lapse $\alpha_{\min}(t)$, minimum conformal factor $\chi_{\min}(t)$, maximum extrinsic curvature $|K|_{\max}(t)$, and a horizon-radius proxy $r_{H}(t)$.  Separately, the plotfile consumer extracts \texttt{areal\_radius.dat}, recording an areal-radius estimate $R_{\rm areal}(t)$ along a preferred shell.  The stability metric uses the full time series from $t=0$ to $t_{\rm final}$.

\subsubsection{Fractional violation terms}

For any quantity $q(t)$ sampled over the run, define a \emph{positive fractional growth} and a \emph{positive fractional drop}:
\begin{align}
    \Delta q_{\rm grow} &\equiv \frac{\max_t q(t) - q(0)}{\max\!\bigl(|q(0)|,\,q_{\rm floor}\bigr)}, &
    \Delta q_{\rm drop} &\equiv \frac{q(0) - \min_t q(t)}{\max\!\bigl(|q(0)|,\,q_{\rm floor}\bigr)},
\end{align}
with each term clipped to be $\ge 0$.  The five geometry-change indicators implemented in code are:
\begin{align}
    \Delta_K &\equiv \Delta\bigl(|K|_{\max}\bigr)_{\rm grow}
    \quad\text{with } q_{\rm floor} = 10^{-1}, \label{eq:delta_k}\\
    \Delta_\alpha &\equiv \Delta\bigl(\alpha_{\min}\bigr)_{\rm drop}
    \quad\text{with } q_{\rm floor} = 10^{-6}, \label{eq:delta_alpha}\\
    \Delta_\chi &\equiv \Delta\bigl(\chi_{\min}\bigr)_{\rm drop}
    \quad\text{with } q_{\rm floor} = 10^{-8}, \label{eq:delta_chi}\\
    \Delta_{r_H} &\equiv \Delta\bigl(r_H\bigr)_{\rm grow}
    \quad\text{with } q_{\rm floor} = 1, \label{eq:delta_rh}\\
    \Delta_{R_{\rm areal}} &\equiv \max_t \frac{\bigl|R_{\rm areal}(t) - R_{\rm areal}(0)\bigr|}{\max\!\bigl(|R_{\rm areal}(0)|,\,10^{-8}\bigr)}. \label{eq:delta_areal}
\end{align}
Physically, $\Delta_K$ penalizes monotonic focusing of extrinsic curvature; $\Delta_\alpha$ and $\Delta_\chi$ penalize lapse and conformal-factor collapse; $\Delta_{r_H}$ flags apparent-horizon growth; and $\Delta_{R_{\rm areal}}$ flags throat or bubble drift.  Terms whose diagnostics are absent are omitted from the sum.

\subsubsection{Geometry-change budget and stability reward}

The violation budget is the unweighted sum of all available terms:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\rm geom}
    =
    \Delta_K + \Delta_\alpha + \Delta_\chi + \Delta_{r_H} + \Delta_{R_{\rm areal}}.
    \label{eq:delta_geom}
\end{equation}
Large $\Delta_{\rm geom}$ means the geometry changed substantially over the evolution window; $\Delta_{\rm geom}\approx 0$ means it remained nearly stationary.  The stability \emph{score component} is the bounded reward from Eq.~\eqref{eq:bounded_reward} with scale $\sigma=1$:
\begin{equation}
    s_{\rm stability} = r(\Delta_{\rm geom};\,1) = \frac{1}{1 + \Delta_{\rm geom}}.
    \label{eq:s_stability}
\end{equation}
This is a \textbf{reward}, not a penalty: $s_{\rm stability}\in(0,1]$, and \textbf{higher values are better}.  A near-equilibrium run has $s_{\rm stability}\to 1$; a rapidly collapsing run drives $s_{\rm stability}\to 0$.  Reference values:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $s_{\rm stability} = 1.0$: no measurable geometry change ($\Delta_{\rm geom}=0$);
    \item $s_{\rm stability} = 0.5$: moderate change ($\Delta_{\rm geom}=1$);
    \item $s_{\rm stability} < 0.25$: flagged in \texttt{score.json} with the note ``geometry changes rapidly over the evolution window.''
\end{itemize}
The total objective adds this component with weight $w_{\rm stability}=2.0$:
\begin{equation}
    S(\theta) = \cdots + 2.0\, s_{\rm stability}(\theta) + \cdots
\end{equation}
CMA-ES maximizes $S$ (equivalently, minimizes $-S$), so low stability directly reduces fitness even when survival and constraint terms remain high.

\subsubsection{Promotion criteria}

A candidate should be promoted only if it (i)~completes the requested run time, (ii)~keeps constraints controlled, and (iii)~maintains a bounded geometry.  Operationally we require $s_{\rm stability}>0.25$ as a minimum gate; a genuine dynamical equilibrium should approach $s_{\rm stability}\gtrsim 0.8$.  Tables~\ref{tab:gpu_radial_smoke}--\ref{tab:amr_optimize} show that reference seeds reach $s_{\rm stability}\sim 0.3$--$0.7$ over $t=2$, while AMR-optimized survivors remain below $0.09$, confirming that the optimizer was still exploiting the stop-time gap.

\section{CMA-ES vs.\ Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:cma_vs_rl}

The current pipeline is black-box evolutionary optimization, not reinforcement learning.  CMA-ES maintains $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{C})$, samples candidates each generation, ranks them by $f(\theta)=-S(\theta)$, and updates $(\mathbf{m}, \mathbf{C}, \sigma)$.  There are no policy networks, value functions, or gradients through the PDE solver.  The ``weights'' are the scoring weights $w_k$ in Table~\ref{tab:score_weights} (user hyperparameters), not neural parameters.

RL would apply only if the problem becomes sequential---e.g., mid-evolution gauge adjustments or an LLM meta-agent choosing basis modes from batch failure statistics (Level~4).  Three distinct weight sets appear in this project: scoring weights $w_k$, CMA-ES search-state $(\mathbf{m}, \mathbf{C})$, and (future) neural-field decoder weights at Level~3.

\section{Avoiding Fake FTL (Operational Probe Tests)}

A major scientific risk in numerical relativity is "fake FTL," where a coordinate transformation (such as a massive shift vector $\beta^i$) makes it look like a region is moving superluminally when it is actually a coordinate effect. 

To eliminate coordinate artifacts, every high-scoring candidate must pass a physical operational test:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item An active probe (either a massless scalar-field pulse or a set of null geodesic tracer particles) is emitted at a boundary $A$.
    \item The pulse propagates through the dynamical spacetime to a detector at boundary $B$.
    \item We compare the arrival time at $B$ with an identical simulation run in flat Minkowski space on the exact same grid.
    \item If the signal arrives earlier, we verify that the signal path did not cross an apparent horizon (trapped surface), and that the constraint violations remained bounded during the entire transit time.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Implemented: Constraint-Satisfying Metric Guesser}

The core technical challenge for the automated search is that arbitrary initial data almost always violates the Einstein constraint equations, causing immediate junk radiation, gauge failure, and solver crashes. We have implemented a six-component pipeline that bridges the gap between random metric proposals and physically meaningful evolutions.

\subsection{Physics of the Constraint Gap}

For the conformally flat recipe ($\tilde\gamma_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$, $A_{ij} = 0$), the Einstein constraints reduce to:
\begin{align}
    R[\chi] + \frac{2}{3}K^2 &= 16\pi\rho, \label{eq:ham_recipe} \\
    -\frac{2}{3}D^i K &= 8\pi j^i. \label{eq:mom_recipe}
\end{align}
For a scalar field with $\Pi = 0$ (momentarily static), the momentum density $j^i = -\Pi\,\partial^i\phi = 0$, so the momentum constraint requires $D^i K = 0$---that is, $K$ must be spatially constant. If the recipe generates spatially varying $K$ with $\Pi = 0$, the momentum constraint is violated. If $\chi$ and $\phi$ are independently random, the Hamiltonian constraint is violated.

\subsection{Component 1: Constrained Recipe (Python Pre-Processing)}
\label{sec:constrained_recipe}

Rather than guessing $\phi$ independently, we derive it from $\chi$ so that the Hamiltonian constraint is approximately satisfied at $t=0$. The implementation (\texttt{constrained\_recipe.py}) performs the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Lock $K$ to a spatial constant (use \texttt{recipe\_K\_asymptotic}, zero out all $K$ Gaussian coefficients). This exactly satisfies the momentum constraint when $\Pi = 0$.
    \item Lock $\Pi = 0$ (zero out all $\Pi$ coefficients).
    \item Evaluate $\chi(r)$ on a fine 1D radial grid from the proposed Gaussian basis coefficients.
    \item Compute the 3-Ricci scalar using $R = -8\psi^{-5}\nabla^2\psi$ where $\psi = \chi^{-1/4}$.
    \item Solve for $\phi(r)$ via radial integration of
    \begin{equation}
        \left(\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right)^2 = s\,\chi\,\frac{R + \frac{2}{3}K^2}{8\pi},
    \end{equation}
    where $s = +1$ for a normal scalar field and $s = -1$ for a phantom field. Regions where the integrand is negative are flagged as unsatisfiable.
    \item Fit the resulting $\phi(r)$ back to Gaussian basis coefficients via least-squares, and inject them into \texttt{params.txt}.
\end{enumerate}

This is activated by the \texttt{--constrained} flag in the CLI. The optimizer then searches only over $\chi$ coefficients and basis parameters; $\phi$ is always derived consistently.

\subsection{Component 2: Pre-Flight Constraint Filter}

Before launching \texttt{GRTeclyn} on the GPU, the wrapper evaluates the Hamiltonian and momentum constraint residuals on a coarse 1D radial grid (\texttt{preflight.py}). Candidates with $\|H\|_{L^2}$ or $\|M_i\|_{L^2}$ exceeding configurable thresholds, or with $\chi < 0$ in more than 10\% of the grid, are rejected without spending GPU time. This is activated by the \texttt{--preflight} flag.

\subsection{Component 3: Energy Density Diagnostic (C++)}

We extended \texttt{RadialRecipeLevel.cpp} to compute the vacuum Hamiltonian constraint (without matter subtraction) at every grid point, giving the \emph{required} energy density:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\rm required}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{16\pi}\left(R + \frac{2}{3}K^2 - A_{ij}A^{ij}\right).
\end{equation}
Three new columns are written to \texttt{constraint\_norms.dat} at every diagnostic step: $\min(\rho_{\rm req})$, $\max(\rho_{\rm req})$, and the integrated volume of negative $\rho_{\rm req}$. These allow the Python scorer to evaluate energy-condition compliance without a separate post-processing pass.

\subsection{Component 4: Enhanced Scoring}

The episode scorer (\texttt{score.py}) combines log-amplified FTL shortcut, expansion asymmetry, non-flat geometry, co-moving and Eulerian stability, survival, constraint health, lapse health, horizon penalty, nontrivial geometry, energy condition, and initial constraint quality (Table~\ref{tab:score_weights}).

\subsection{Component 5: CMA-ES Optimization Driver}

The random atlas search has been supplemented with a CMA-ES (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy) optimizer (\texttt{optimize.py}). The default search space consists of the four $\chi$ Gaussian basis coefficients and the basis width (5 dimensions). Each evaluation runs a full \texttt{GRTeclyn} episode and returns the negative total score. The optimizer is invoked via:
\begin{verbatim}
python -m grteclyn_wrapper \
    --example RadialRecipe \
    --constrained --preflight optimize \
    --max-generations 50 --seed 42
\end{verbatim}
CMA-ES automatically adjusts the search distribution based on the fitness landscape, concentrating evaluations in promising regions of the coefficient space.

\subsection{Component 6: Known-Solution Seeds}

Three analytically known spacetimes are provided as regression benchmarks and optimizer warm-start points (\texttt{seeds.py}):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Flat Minkowski}: $\chi = 1$, all coefficients zero. All constraints are exactly satisfied; the evolution should remain static to machine precision.
    \item \textbf{Ellis--Bronnikov wormhole}: The exact isotropic $\chi$ and $\phi$ profiles (Eqs.~7 and 11) are fitted to the Gaussian basis via least-squares. This verifies that the recipe basis can faithfully represent a known exotic geometry.
    \item \textbf{Schwarzschild puncture}: The Brill--Lindquist conformal factor $\psi = 1 + M/(2\bar{r})$ fitted to Gaussians. A vacuum solution that tests the recipe's ability to handle steep $\chi$ gradients near a singularity.
\end{enumerate}

These seeds are selected via \texttt{--seed-name} and can be combined with the optimizer to search for perturbations around known solutions. Validation-batch IDs (\texttt{random\_000}, \texttt{bubble\_wall\_016}, \ldots) are selected via \texttt{--candidate-id}; non-spherical proposals via \texttt{--nonspherical-id} (see Sec.~\ref{sec:gpu_nonspherical}).

\subsection{Component 7: Plotfile extraction and frame cleanup}

The runtime now treats AMReX plotfiles as transient data products rather than permanent artifacts.  During each GPU episode, \texttt{consume\_plotfiles.py} extracts shell profiles, areal-radius diagnostics, and centered PNG frames for \texttt{chi}, \texttt{lapse}, and \texttt{K}.  The heavy \texttt{RadialRecipePlt*} directories are deleted after successful extraction.

Two implementation details are important for robust cluster operation.  First, the live plotfile consumer renders frames before deletion, while the post-run drain uses \texttt{--keep-existing-frames} and \texttt{--stable-seconds 0} so that late plotfiles are appended rather than clearing earlier frames.  Second, the wrapper launches the live consumer in its own process group and terminates that group after the C++ evolution finishes.  This prevents orphaned worker processes from holding deleted HDF5 files open as NFS \texttt{.nfs*} busy files.

\subsection{Verification Tests}

All six components are verified by a saved Python test suite:

\end{verbatim}

The script exercises five groups of checks (74 assertions total) before any GPU time is spent.

\subsubsection{Test groups}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Known analytical seeds} (6 cases): flat Minkowski, Ellis--Bronnikov wormholes at $b_0 \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$, and Schwarzschild punctures at $M \in \{0.5, 1.0\}$.
    \item \textbf{Random $\chi$ profiles, normal field} (8 cases): $\chi$ coefficients drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.15^2)$ with $N \in [3,8]$, basis width $w \in [0.3, 2.5]$, and $K \in [-0.3, 0.3]$.
    \item \textbf{Random $\chi$ profiles, phantom field} (8 cases): same layout with larger $\chi$ perturbations and $K \in [-0.5, 0.5]$.
    \item \textbf{Edge cases} (7 cases): very narrow/wide basis, $N=1$ and $N=12$, nearly singular $\chi$, non-zero $K$, depressed asymptotic $\chi$.
    \item \textbf{Constraint improvement} (5 paired trials): compare pre-flight Hamiltonian $L^2$ before and after \texttt{constrained\_overrides}, with random independent $\phi$, $K$, and $\Pi$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Results summary}

On the reference run (\texttt{numpy 2.4.6}, seed 42): \textbf{74/74 checks passed}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Robustness}: all 16 random profiles and all 7 edge cases produced finite $\phi$ coefficients with bounded Gaussian fit residuals ($< 1$). No NaNs or crashes.
    \item \textbf{Flat Minkowski}: $\phi$ coefficients remain exactly zero; pre-flight passes with $\|H\|_{L^2} = 0$.
    \item \textbf{Momentum constraint}: locking $K$ to a spatial constant and $\Pi = 0$ drives $\|M_i\|_{L^2} = 0$ in all five improvement trials.
    \item \textbf{Hamiltonian improvement}: in 4/5 random trials, constrained overrides reduced $\|H\|_{L^2}$ (best case: $3.9 \times 10^{-1} \to 2.2 \times 10^{-1}$). One trial regressed slightly ($2.5 \times 10^{-1} \to 2.7 \times 10^{-1}$, $\sim 7\%$) due to Gaussian fit error in the derived $\phi$; the test suite allows up to 15\% regression to account for this discretisation noise.
    \item \textbf{Pathological rejection}: deliberately bad inputs ($\chi_0 = -5$, spatially varying $K$) are rejected by pre-flight with multiple independent criteria.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Known limitations (expected behaviour)}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{EB $\phi$ recovery is approximate, not exact}: re-deriving $\phi$ from the Gaussian-fitted $\chi$ via finite-difference Ricci integration yields $\max|\phi_{\rm orig} - \phi_{\rm derived}| \approx 0.2$ for $b_0 = 0.5$. The seed's $\phi$ was fit analytically; the constraint solver recomputes from the discrete $\chi$ profile, so throat-resolution error is expected.
    \item \textbf{Wide-throat wormholes fail pre-flight}: Ellis--Bronnikov seeds with $b_0 \ge 1.0$ report $\|H\|_{L^2} \sim 10^2$--$10^2$ on the coarse 1D grid. Steep throat curvature exceeds what 8--12 Gaussians can represent; more basis functions or narrower widths are needed.
    \item \textbf{Vacuum Schwarzschild fails pre-flight without matter}: puncture $\chi$ alone cannot satisfy $H = 0$ with $\phi = 0$ on the recipe's conformally flat ansatz. Pre-flight correctly flags $\|H\|_{L^2} \sim 10^1$--$10^2$. Full 3D constraint solving (e.g.\ \texttt{GRTresna}) remains required for vacuum BH initial data.
    \item \textbf{Pre-flight is a heuristic filter, not a proof}: the 1D radial check uses 512--2048 points and finite-difference derivatives. The definitive constraint evaluation happens in the C++ \texttt{RadialRecipeLevel} diagnostic loop on the full 3D grid.
\end{enumerate}

These limitations define the intended operating envelope: the constrained recipe is a fast \emph{first pass} that eliminates the worst violations and locks the momentum constraint, while the C++ evolution and scoring pipeline provide the authoritative physics verdict.

uv run python -m grteclyn_wrapper validate \

\end{verbatim}

\subsubsection{What it does}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Generates a fixed deterministic candidate set (seed 42): 12 random Gaussian profiles, 3 shells, 3 Alcubierre-inspired bubble walls, 3 multi-shell profiles, 3 pathological cases, and 3 bad controls.
    \item Validates each candidate \emph{raw} (random independent $\phi$, $K$, $\Pi$) and \emph{constrained} (derived $\phi$, locked $K$, $\Pi=0$).

    \item Classifies each outcome with an explicit label and reason.
    \item Writes \texttt{guesser\_validation.csv} and \texttt{guesser\_validation\_summary.json}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{How the initial $\chi(r)$ profile is drawn}

The metric guesser starts by proposing the conformal factor $\chi(r)$ on a radial grid. In the C++ recipe this is represented by a Gaussian radial basis:

\begin{equation}
    \chi(r) = \chi_\infty + \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} c_n
    \exp\left[-\frac{(r-r_n)^2}{2w^2}\right],
\end{equation}

where $\chi_\infty$ is the asymptotic value (usually 1), $c_n$ are the guessed coefficients, $w$ is the shared basis width, and the nodes $r_n$ are uniformly spaced from $0$ to \texttt{recipe\_basis\_radius\_max}. This is the actual shape that \texttt{RadialRecipeInitialData} places on the AMReX grid.

The validation harness draws several families of $\chi(r)$:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Gaussian profiles}: directly sample the coefficients $c_n$ from a controlled normal distribution.
    \item \textbf{Shells}: build a target radial shell, then fit it back into the Gaussian basis.
    \item \textbf{Bubble walls}: use an Alcubierre-style top-hat shape function only as a radial wall proxy, then fit the wall into the Gaussian basis.
    \item \textbf{Multi-shells}: combine two radial shells to test more complex geometries.
    \item \textbf{Pathological/bad controls}: deliberately create steep gradients, narrow basis functions, negative $\chi$, spatially varying $K$, or random $\Pi$.
\end{itemize}

After $\chi(r)$ is drawn, the constrained recipe does \emph{not} guess $\phi(r)$ independently. Instead, it computes the Ricci scalar from $\chi$, solves the Hamiltonian constraint for $\partial_r\phi$, integrates to obtain $\phi(r)$, and fits that result back into the same Gaussian basis.

\subsubsection{Current symmetry limitation}

The first-stage metric guesser is intentionally spherically symmetric. All guessed fields depend only on the radius,
\begin{equation}
    \chi=\chi(r), \qquad K=K(r), \qquad \phi=\phi(r), \qquad \Pi=\Pi(r),
\end{equation}
with $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$. The spatial metric ansatz is therefore conformally flat and radial,
\begin{equation}
    \gamma_{ij} = \chi(r)^{-1}\delta_{ij}.
\end{equation}
This means the current search can draw smooth spherical blobs, shells, and radial bubble-wall proxies, but it cannot yet represent tilted bubbles, rotating structures, multiple separated mouths, or a true moving Alcubierre bubble.

This restriction is deliberate for the first validation stage: radial symmetry makes it easier to test the constraint solver, energy diagnostics, Gaussian basis fitting, and the Python-to-C++ handoff. Once the present constrained radial solution is validated dynamically, the next symmetry-breaking extension should introduce angular modes, for example spherical harmonics,
\begin{equation}
    \chi(r,\theta,\varphi)
    =
    \chi_0(r)
    +
    \sum_{\ell,m} c_{\ell m}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi).
\end{equation}
This would allow lopsided bubbles, multi-mouth portal-like structures, and genuinely non-spherical warp geometries.

\subsubsection{First non-spherical ray validation}

As a first implementation step toward symmetry breaking, the Python wrapper now supports axisymmetric angular deformations of the radial conformal factor:
\begin{equation}
    \chi(r,\theta)
    =
    \chi_0(r)
    +
    \sum_a
    A_a
    \exp\left[-\frac{(r-r_a)^2}{2w_a^2}\right]
    P_{\ell_a}(\cos\theta),
\end{equation}
where $P_\ell$ are Legendre polynomials. The same ansatz is now implemented in \texttt{RadialRecipeInitialData} (C++) via \texttt{recipe\_num\_chi\_angular\_modes} and per-mode keys \texttt{recipe\_chi\_mode\_ell\_*}, \texttt{recipe\_chi\_mode\_amp\_*}, \texttt{recipe\_chi\_mode\_rc\_*}, \texttt{recipe\_chi\_mode\_rw\_*}. A complementary path uses full scalar spherical harmonics $Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)$ through \texttt{recipe\_num\_chi\_Ylm\_modes} and \texttt{recipe\_chi\_Ylm\_l\_*}, \texttt{recipe\_chi\_Ylm\_m\_*}, etc., evaluated with the existing \texttt{SphericalHarmonics::spin\_Y\_lm} routine ($s=0$).

The first validation is a one-step metric sanity check along several 1D radial rays ($\theta=0,\pi/4,\pi/2,3\pi/4,\pi$). For each candidate we measure:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the minimum and maximum $\chi$ across all sampled rays;
    \item the fraction of ray samples with $\chi\le0$;
    \item the maximum angular contrast $\max_\theta\chi(r,\theta)-\min_\theta\chi(r,\theta)$;
    \item the corresponding radial stretch range $a(r,\theta)=\chi(r,\theta)^{-1/2}$.
\end{itemize}

The command is:
\begin{verbatim}
cd grteclyn-wrapper
uv run python tests/validate_nonspherical_guesser.py \
    --output-dir validation_out
\end{verbatim}

The reference run (seed 42) produced 8 non-spherical candidates: 7 were classified as \texttt{accepted\_ray\_sane}, and 1 deliberately strong quadrupole bad-control was rejected as \texttt{rejected\_negative\_chi}. The validation files are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \path|nonspherical_ray_validation.csv|
    \item \path|nonspherical_ray_validation_summary.json|
\end{itemize}

This ray validation only checks whether the non-spherical metric proposal remains positive and geometrically reasonable along sampled directions. It does \emph{not} solve the non-spherical Hamiltonian or momentum constraints on its own. The GPU results in Sec.~\ref{sec:gpu_nonspherical} show that ray-sane candidates also survive short 3D \texttt{RadialRecipe} evolution once the constrained $\phi$ recipe and C++ angular initial data are enabled.

\section{Forward Path: From Stability Gap to Analytic Discovery}
\label{sec:forward_path}

The closed-loop pipeline has executed its first optimization campaigns (Sec.~\ref{sec:gpu_radial_smoke}, Table~\ref{tab:amr_optimize}).  The central remaining challenge is the \textbf{dynamical stability gap}: even with $s_{\rm stability}$ implemented, initial runs show optimizers favoring geometries that collapse slowly enough to outlive a short stop time.  The path to a production-grade tool proceeds in four phases.

\subsection{Phase 1: Closing the stability gap (immediate software priority)}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tune stability scoring.}  The current $\Delta_{\rm geom}$ sum is live in \texttt{score.py} (weight 2.0).  Next: calibrate scales, extend stop times ($t=5$--$10$), and require $s_{\rm stability}>0.25$ for promotion.
    \item \textbf{FTL profile scoring (upgraded).}  $F_{\rm log}$, $F_{\rm asymmetry}$, and $s_{\rm comoving}$ are active in \texttt{ftl\_metrics.py} and \texttt{score.py} (Secs.~\ref{sec:ftl_scaffolding}--\ref{sec:comoving_scoring}); GPU validation in Tables~\ref{tab:gpu_ftl_upgraded}--\ref{tab:gpu_ftl_nonspherical}.  Alcubierre warp now outranks flat Minkowski ($S=10.08$ vs.\ $10.00$).  Next tier: dynamic axis-probe confirmation after evolution.
    \item \textbf{Basis and width sweeps.}  Automated sweeps over $N\in[4,16]$ and $w\in[0.3,2.5]$ map the fit residual $\epsilon_{\rm fit}$ and set the minimum safe CMA-ES dimensionality.
    \item \textbf{Gauge stability to $t=5$.}  Extend the seven non-spherical survivors (Table~\ref{tab:gpu_nonspherical}) and promoted radial seeds through $t=5$ on the $N_{\rm full}=64,96,128$ ladder to verify $1+\log$ slicing and Gamma-driver shift under deformed geometry.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase 2: Multi-observer energy conditions}

The pipeline currently tests $\rho_{\rm req}\ge 0$ in the Eulerian frame only---a coordinate loophole.  Extend \texttt{RadialRecipeLevel.cpp} to build a local tetrad and contract $T_{\hat\mu\hat\nu}$ against timelike and null observer velocities:
\begin{equation}
    \Xi_W(\mathbf{x}) = T_{\hat{\mu}\hat{\nu}}\,V^{\hat{\mu}}V^{\hat{\nu}}.
\end{equation}
Feed the minimum over sampled observers into \texttt{score.py} so candidates cannot look physical to a stationary observer but exotic to one moving with the bubble.

\subsection{Phase 3: GPU-cluster production search}

With physics and scoring locked down:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{1000-candidate constraint atlas.}  Batch Slurm job with \texttt{--constrained --preflight} to populate \texttt{atlas.csv}---a structured ``Spacetime Failure Atlas'' for training Level~4 research agents.
    \item \textbf{50-generation CMA-ES campaign.}
\begin{verbatim}
python -m grteclyn_wrapper --example RadialRecipe \
    --constrained --preflight optimize \
    --max-generations 50 --seed 42
\end{verbatim}
    With exact momentum constraint ($K=\mathrm{const}$, $\Pi=0$) and approximate Hamiltonian satisfaction at $t=0$, the optimizer can focus entirely on stable, FTL-capable shapes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Phase 4: Analytical back-derivation}

A numerical optimum is a coefficient list, not a paper-ready metric.  Once a stable survivor emerges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Reconstruct $\chi_{\rm num}(r)$ from \texttt{params.txt} and feed it to symbolic regression (PySR / Eureqa), searching forms such as power laws, $\mathrm{sech}^2$ walls, or rational profiles (target $\epsilon<10^{-4}$).
    \item Solve the Hamiltonian constraint analytically for $\phi(r)$ from the closed-form $\chi(r)$.
    \item Reload the analytic profile in \texttt{GRTeclyn} via a custom initial-data module and verify stability and energy conditions match the numerical discovery---completing the cycle from automated search to exact analytical solutions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This project moves interstellar metric search from static, hand-written ans\"atze toward dynamic, GPU-driven discovery.  The pipeline now closes the loop from Python parameter proposals through constrained C++ evolution, stability-aware scoring, and plotfile extraction.  Tables~\ref{tab:gpu_radial_smoke}--\ref{tab:amr_optimize} quantify the stability gap quantitatively: reference seeds score $s_{\rm stability}\sim 0.3$--$0.7$ over $t=2$, while AMR-optimized candidates remain below $0.09$.  Phase~1--4 above turn this working prototype into a rigorous map of physical constructibility boundaries and, ultimately, a path to new exact solutions in general relativity.
